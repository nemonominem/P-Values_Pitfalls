# Article Review: p-values and other significance pitfalls

- date: 2026-05-01
- reviewer: Raptor mini (Preview)
- subject: Fact-check review of `p-values-pitfalls.md`

## Summary

The article is largely aligned with standard critiques of p-values and the use of False Positive Risk (FPR) as a complement. The main claims about misinterpretations, the transposed conditional problem, the need for a second step beyond the p-value, and the recommendation to treat `0.005–0.05` as "suggestive" are supported by existing literature.

## Verified points

- The article correctly flags the common p-value misinterpretations.
- The prosecutor's-fallacy / transposed conditional argument is valid.
- The idea that p-values alone are insufficient and that p-value + FPR is a better single-experiment inference strategy is consistent with Colquhoun and related work.
- The Bayes factor upper bound formula is real and widely cited in the literature.
- The specific numeric examples in section 1.4.d are correct for the assumed well-powered two-sample setup:
  - `p = 0.05`, prior = `0.5` → `FPR ≈ 0.2662`
  - `p = 0.0079`, prior = `0.5` → `FPR ≈ 0.0498`
  - `p = 0.005`, prior = `0.5` → `FPR ≈ 0.0338`
  - `p = 0.005`, prior = `0.1` → `FPR ≈ 0.2395`
  - `p = 0.001`, prior = `0.1` → `FPR ≈ 0.0829`
- The p-value needed to reach `FPR = 0.05` with prior `0.5` is about `0.00794`.
- The p-value needed to reach `FPR = 0.05` with prior `0.1` is about `0.000449`.

## Issue found

- The statement that a two-sided `p = 0.01` with prior `0.5` gives an FPR of `~11%` is not supported under the same well-powered calculation. With the assumed setup, `p = 0.01` and prior `0.5` give an FPR closer to `6%`.

  This suggests the `11%` figure either comes from a different prior/power assumption or from a different definition of false-positive rate.

## Recommendation

- Correct or clarify the `p = 0.01` / `11%` claim.
- Optionally clarify terminology around `FPR` vs `FDR`, since the article currently uses them interchangeably.
